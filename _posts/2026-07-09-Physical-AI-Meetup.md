Attended the **Physical AI Meetup** in Perth on 9 July 2026.

The event brought together people working across AI, hardware, mining, robotics, and research. It was hosted with support from the Curtin Institute for Data Science, and the evening included three talks, an open mic, and time to meet other people in the local AI community.

What I liked most was how different the three talks were. Each one showed a completely different side of applied AI.

The first talk, by **Ayrton Sue from Element Engineering**, focused on the importance of choosing the right level of technology for a real business problem. The main idea was not to start with the most advanced solution, but to first understand what the client actually needs and then use the simplest technology that can deliver value quickly.

He moved across the spectrum from basic sensors to more advanced robotics and humanoid systems. What stood out to me was how important hardware availability and manufacturing infrastructure are.

In the United States, many parts are easier to source and build into products, while in Australia the process can be much more difficult. It made me wonder why Australia has not developed a more complete manufacturing ecosystem, with stronger supply chains and production capability similar to what exists in countries such as China.

The talk made it clear that physical AI is not only about models or software. Hardware, manufacturing, logistics, and supply chains are just as important.

The second talk, by **Tika Elliot from Augment**, focused on blast movement in open-pit mining.

Every blast moves ore from its original location, and if that movement is not understood properly, valuable material can be lost or mixed with waste. The company uses physics-driven AI modelling to estimate how the ore moves and help mining teams reduce ore loss and dilution.

This talk felt especially relatable because it connected with areas I have already studied, including machine learning, data engineering, modelling, and scientific computing. It was interesting to see how these ideas are applied to a very practical mining problem with real economic impact.

The final talk, by **Rakib Hasan from Curtin University**, explored trustworthy AI for empathy detection in text and video.

One line that stayed with me was:

> What is measured can be improved.

Empathy is difficult to define and even harder to measure, but that does not mean it should be ignored. The talk looked at how language and video models could detect empathy while also considering uncertainty, privacy, fairness, and reliability.

I found this topic particularly interesting because empathy may become increasingly important as AI systems become more capable and interact with people more naturally. If we eventually move toward more advanced forms of AI, those systems will need to deal with human emotions carefully, not just generate technically correct answers.

Trustworthy AI is therefore not only about accuracy. It is also about knowing when a model is uncertain, avoiding unfair outcomes, protecting privacy, and understanding the limits of what the system can infer.

The evening ended with a short community open mic and more networking.

Overall, the meetup was a valuable experience. It reminded me that AI is not one single field. It connects software, hardware, data, physics, psychology, engineering, and business.

The three talks also gave me three different ideas to think about:

- advanced technology is not always the right starting point
- AI modelling becomes most valuable when it solves a real industry problem
- trustworthy AI needs to consider human values, not only model performance

It was another good opportunity to learn from people working in industry and research, and to see how broad the AI community in Perth has become.
