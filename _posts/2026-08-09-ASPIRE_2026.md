This was my first time finishing a competition with four awards.

I joined **IEEE Curtin ASPIRE 2026**, a two-week international innovation and sustainability challenge involving students from Curtin University's Bentley, Dubai, Colombo, and Malaysia campuses.

Our team eventually developed **Aerviora**, a personalised environmental decision-support web application designed to help people interpret environmental conditions and make better-informed decisions about outdoor activities.

The technical outcome was satisfying, but one of the biggest things I took away from the competition had much less to do with code.

It was learning how to actually deliver something with people who were working from different countries, different campuses, and different time zones.

## A competition across four Curtin campuses

IEEE Curtin ASPIRE 2026 was jointly organised by the IEEE Student Branches at Curtin University **Bentley, Dubai, Colombo, and Malaysia**.

The competition was designed around cross-campus collaboration. Students were placed into international teams and given two weeks to identify a real problem, connect it to the UN Sustainable Development Goals, develop a feasible solution, build a prototype, and present it to judges.

The structure was roughly divided into two stages:

- **Week 1 - Ideation Sprint**
- **Week 2 - Build Sprint**

The first week focused on understanding the problem, validating the idea, thinking about users, sustainability, feasibility, and the business model.

The second week was about turning that idea into something we could actually demonstrate.

That sounded straightforward when I first joined.

The first practical problem, however, appeared before we had even decided what to build.

We had to find a time when everyone could meet.

## The first challenge was simply finding a time to talk

The entire competition was conducted online.

Our team members were spread across several time zones:

```text
Perth / Bentley    AWST   UTC+8
Malaysia           MYT    UTC+8
Sri Lanka          SLST   UTC+5:30
Dubai              GST    UTC+4
```

Perth and Malaysia were easy because we were on the same UTC offset.

Sri Lanka was two and a half hours behind us, and Dubai was four hours behind.

A meeting that felt perfectly normal at 7:30 pm in Perth could therefore be 3:30 pm in Dubai. A morning meeting in Sri Lanka could become lunchtime for us.

At the beginning, even arranging the group discussion required some thought.

We used the WhatsApp group created by the organisers for quick communication, Microsoft Teams for meetings, and shared online tools for the actual project work.

One of the useful ideas was to stop repeatedly asking:

> "Are you free at this time?"

Instead, Nath created a shared Excel availability sheet.

Each person could mark the times they were available in their own region, while the sheet made it easier for everyone else to compare that against their local time.

![Cross-time-zone availability sheet](/images/2026-08-09-ASPIRE2026/excel-sheet.png)

It was a very simple tool, but it solved a real coordination problem.

We also discussed using polls when necessary and tried to communicate meeting times with the relevant time zones written beside them.

Something like:

```text
11:00 am Sri Lanka
1:30 pm Perth / Malaysia
9:30 am Dubai
```

became much safer than sending a message saying only "Let's meet at 1:30".

That small habit probably saved us from a lot of confusion.

## Actually working as a global team

Once we could reliably meet, the next challenge was making the online collaboration useful.

We used shared Miro boards for brainstorming, research, user problems, solution ideas, and business-model discussions.

![Aerviora team collaboration on Miro](/images/2026-08-09-ASPIRE2026/miro.png)

We also used Microsoft Teams meetings to discuss the idea, divide work, test the prototype, and prepare the final presentation.

![Online team meeting during ASPIRE 2026](/images/2026-08-09-ASPIRE2026/teams-meeting.png)

I had worked on group projects before, but this felt different.

There was no physical classroom where everyone would automatically appear at the same time.

There was no easy way to turn to the person next to me and ask what they were doing.

If something was unclear, we had to communicate it.

If someone was working on a section, the rest of the team needed enough visibility to know how it fitted into the whole project.

If a deadline was approaching, we had to decide what was essential and what could wait.

That made the competition a surprisingly useful exercise in **global project management, remote collaboration, and leadership**.

Leadership in this situation did not mean telling everyone what to do.

A lot of it was much more practical:

- making sure everyone knew what the current goal was
- breaking large tasks into pieces
- checking whether people were blocked
- keeping the prototype, pitch, research, and business story aligned
- changing direction when an idea was too large for the time available
- making sure technical details could be understood by non-technical team members

I think that last point became especially important later.

## From an idea to Aerviora

Our early discussions were much broader than the final product.

We explored problems around extreme heat, humidity, dust, air quality, respiratory sensitivity, and the difficulty of deciding whether outdoor conditions were suitable for a particular person and activity.

One useful observation was that the environmental data itself already existed.

Weather applications can show temperature.

Air-quality services can show particulate pollution.

UV forecasts can show UV levels.

The problem was that a user still had to look at several numbers and decide:

> What does this actually mean for me, doing this activity, for this amount of time?

That became the core idea behind **Aerviora**.

Instead of trying to build another general weather application, we focused on a decision-support layer.

Aerviora combines environmental conditions with user context such as sensitivity, activity, and expected outdoor duration, then produces an explainable concern level, practical guidance, and a personalised outlook.

The prototype eventually included:

- live weather and air-quality data
- temperature, apparent temperature, humidity, UV, PM2.5, PM10, and modelled dust
- user-selected environmental sensitivities
- activity and expected exposure duration
- a deterministic rule-based decision engine
- explanations showing what drove each recommendation
- preparation suggestions
- lower-risk timing recommendations
- Day, 3-Day, and Week personalised outlooks
- an explicit demo mode for reliable presentation scenarios
- a privacy-focused design with no persistent health profile

The final version was built with **Next.js, React, TypeScript, and Tailwind CSS**, using Open-Meteo weather and air-quality services with modelled Copernicus CAMS data.

One design decision I was particularly happy with was keeping the main recommendation engine deterministic rather than adding generative AI simply because AI was available.

For a health-related decision-support prototype, I wanted the result to be explainable and repeatable.

The same inputs should produce the same result, and we should be able to explain why.

## Idea matters, but delivery matters too

One lesson I have gradually learned from hackathons, university projects, research work, and internships is that a good idea is not enough.

**Delivery matters.**

I saw the same thing during my Pawsey internship.

Technical work can become very detailed very quickly. You may understand the implementation, the architecture, and why a particular decision makes sense.

But if you cannot break it down for a broader audience, much of that work becomes invisible.

A technically strong project can still be difficult to evaluate if the audience cannot quickly understand:

- what problem it solves
- who needs it
- how it works
- why the approach is different
- whether the prototype actually works
- what its limitations are
- why the idea matters

ASPIRE reinforced that lesson.

We had only **eight minutes** for the final pitch.

Eight minutes sounds reasonable until you try to fit in:

- the problem
- user evidence
- the solution
- the working prototype
- technical architecture
- innovation
- SDG impact
- business feasibility
- future direction
- conclusion

while also making the presentation feel like one coherent story.

The goal was not to say everything we knew.

The goal was to communicate the most important things clearly enough that the judges understood the project.

## Practising an eight-minute pitch

The final presentation consisted of an **8-minute pitch**, followed by **5 minutes of Q&A**.

We spent a lot of time practising for those eight minutes.

On Friday and Saturday before the final, we ran through the pitch again and again.

Sometimes the presentation was too long.

Sometimes one section contained too much detail.

Sometimes a transition felt awkward.

Sometimes the demo explanation repeated what was already visible on the screen.

Sometimes a technically correct sentence simply took too long to say.

So we kept cutting.

We changed wording.

We timed each speaker.

We decided which technical details belonged in the pitch and which were better saved for Q&A.

We practised how to hand over naturally between speakers.

We prepared for questions about environmental data accuracy, privacy, medical validation, competitors, the decision engine, demo data, scalability, and the difference between Aerviora and a normal weather application.

The recorded prototype demonstration also became useful because it removed some of the uncertainty of relying entirely on a live application during a short competition pitch.

The more we practised, the more I realised that presentation preparation is not separate from technical work.

It is another form of engineering the final product.

You are taking something complicated and deciding what information the audience actually needs.

## The result

After two weeks of research, meetings, implementation, testing, design work, documentation, business-model discussion, and a lot of pitch practice, we presented Aerviora in the Grand Final.

Our team received four awards:

- **Overall Champion**
- **Best Pitch**
- **Best Technical Design**
- **Most Innovative Solutions**

![IEEE Curtin ASPIRE 2026 awards](/images/2026-08-09-ASPIRE2026/awards.png)


The awards were exciting, but I think I was even more satisfied by how much the project changed during those two weeks.

It started as a rough environmental-health idea on an online whiteboard.

By the end, we had a working application, a tested decision engine, a technical architecture, a live deployment, a recorded demonstration, a business story, and an eight-minute pitch that the whole team could deliver together.

## What I learned about communication

The strongest lesson for me was probably the importance of communication.

Not just public speaking.

Communication inside the team mattered just as much.

When people are distributed across countries and time zones, assumptions become expensive.

A task that seems obvious to one person may not be obvious to someone who missed a meeting.

A technical feature may sound complete to a developer while a teammate preparing the business section still does not know how to describe its value.

A presentation slide may look good individually but duplicate what the next speaker is already saying.

The project worked better whenever we made the current state visible and explained decisions clearly.

I also became more conscious of how I explain technical systems.

For example, there is a very detailed technical explanation of Aerviora's provider normalisation, client/server boundaries, data-readiness logic, risk domains, and forecast algorithms.

That level of detail belongs in documentation.

During an eight-minute pitch, however, the same architecture might need to become:

> Environmental data comes in, it is validated and normalised, and then a deterministic decision engine combines it with the user's context to produce transparent, personalised guidance.

Both explanations are correct.

The skill is knowing which one the audience needs.

## What I learned about scope

Two weeks is enough time to build a surprising amount.

It is also short enough that bad scope decisions become painful very quickly.

At different points we discussed many possible extensions:

- pollen data
- hyper-local sensors
- mapping
- city dashboards
- saved user profiles
- feedback-based learning
- additional environmental conditions
- more advanced personalisation

Some of those could be valuable later.

Trying to build all of them during the competition would probably have made the final product worse.

The most useful decision was to keep returning to the core question:

> What does the user need to decide, and what do we need to demonstrate that this idea works?

That helped us prioritise the end-to-end experience instead of accumulating disconnected features.

I think this is something I want to carry into future projects.

A smaller product that works, can be explained, and has clear limitations is often stronger than a huge concept where every feature is only half implemented.

## A project I will remember

I originally joined IEEE Curtin ASPIRE 2026 because it looked like an interesting international innovation event.

I left with much more than another project for GitHub.

Working with teammates across different countries and time zones, tackling a problem connected to the UN Sustainable Development Goals, and taking an idea from an online brainstorming board to a working prototype and final pitch made this a genuinely meaningful challenge.

It gave me practical experience in global collaboration, project management, leadership, technical delivery, and communicating technical ideas to a broader audience.

And it reminded me again that a project is not finished when the code works.

It is finished when the team can **deliver it clearly, confidently, and together**.

Winning the competition and receiving four awards was an amazing result, but the experience of building and delivering the project together is probably what I will remember longest.

I am very grateful to my teammates, the IEEE Curtin ASPIRE organising team, Curtin University, the workshop mentors, and Senuda Jayasooriya and Umanga Pilapitiya for their time, guidance, and feedback throughout the challenge.

It was a very good two weeks.

And somehow, we finished as Overall Champion with four awards. Cool!😊
