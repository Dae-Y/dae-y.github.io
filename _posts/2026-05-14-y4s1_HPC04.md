While studying HPC, I got really interested in how supercomputers, quantum computing, and cybersecurity all connect.

At first, I had this simple question: if quantum computers keep getting better, does that mean supercomputers could be at risk?

But the real issue isn’t quantum computers directly breaking into HPC systems. It’s that powerful quantum computers might be able to break the public‑key cryptography that modern systems rely on.

A lot of our authentication and encryption methods are based on math that’s hard for classical computers, but could become much easier for quantum ones.


## Quantum Computing and the Security Problem

Modern public-key cryptography often relies on problems such as integer factorisation and discrete logarithms.

For example, RSA is based on the difficulty of factoring a large number:

$ N = pq $

Here, $p$ and $q$ are large prime numbers. The public key includes $N$, but the private key depends on knowing $p$ and $q$. For a classical computer, factoring a sufficiently large $N$ is extremely difficult, which is why RSA has been useful for secure communication.

However, quantum computers change this assumption. Shor’s algorithm shows that a sufficiently capable quantum computer could factor large integers efficiently. This means RSA and related systems would no longer provide the same level of security in a post-quantum world.

This is the reason why people talk about the risk of quantum computers to encryption. It is not mainly about attacking a machine directly, but about weakening the mathematical foundation of existing cryptographic systems.

## Why Shor’s Algorithm Matters

Shor’s algorithm is important because it turns the problem of factoring into a problem of finding a period.

The key idea can be written using a function such as:

$ f(x) = a^x \bmod N $

This function has a repeating pattern. If the period is $r$, then:

$ a^r \equiv 1 \pmod{N} $

Once this period $r$ is found, it can be used to recover the factors of $N$ under the right conditions. One important step is:

$ a^r - 1 = (a^{r/2} - 1)(a^{r/2} + 1) $

Then the factors can be extracted using greatest common divisors:

$ p = \gcd(a^{r/2} - 1, N) $

$ q = \gcd(a^{r/2} + 1, N) $

The quantum part of Shor’s algorithm is powerful because quantum computation can find the period efficiently using the Quantum Fourier Transform. This is why RSA, ECC, and other cryptographic systems based on similar mathematical assumptions are considered vulnerable to future large-scale quantum computers.

## Harvest Now, Decrypt Later

One concept I found especially important is **harvest now, decrypt later**.

The idea is simple: an attacker could collect encrypted data today and store it. Even if they cannot decrypt it now, they may be able to decrypt it later when large-scale quantum computers become available.

This matters for data that needs to remain confidential for many years, such as:

- government records
- health data
- research data
- financial data
- long-term intellectual property

For HPC environments, this is relevant because supercomputing centres often handle valuable research data and sensitive collaborations. Even if current systems remain secure today, long-lived encrypted data needs to be considered from a future-risk perspective.

## What Post-Quantum Cryptography Is

Post-Quantum Cryptography, or **PQC**, refers to cryptographic algorithms designed to remain secure even against quantum computers.

PQC does not usually require quantum hardware. It is still classical cryptography, but based on mathematical problems that are believed to resist known quantum attacks.

The key point is that PQC avoids the structures that Shor’s algorithm is good at attacking.

RSA and ECC rely on mathematical structures with periodicity. Shor’s algorithm can exploit this. In contrast, many PQC schemes are based on problems such as lattice problems, hash-based signatures, or code-based cryptography, where Shor’s period-finding approach does not directly apply.

## Major PQC Families

The main families of post-quantum cryptography include:

- **Lattice-based cryptography**  
  This includes schemes such as ML-KEM and ML-DSA, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber and CRYSTALS-Dilithium. These are based on hard lattice problems such as Learning With Errors.

- **Hash-based signatures**  
  This includes SLH-DSA, derived from SPHINCS+. It is based on the security of hash functions.

- **Code-based cryptography**  
  This includes schemes such as Classic McEliece, based on the difficulty of decoding certain error-correcting codes.

The most important practical point is that standardisation is already happening. NIST published its first post-quantum cryptography standards in 2024, including standards for key establishment and digital signatures.

## Why PQC Is Not Broken by Shor’s Algorithm

The reason PQC is considered resistant to Shor’s algorithm is that it is not based on integer factorisation or discrete logarithms.

Shor’s algorithm is powerful, but it is not a universal algorithm that breaks all cryptography. It is especially effective against problems with the right algebraic and periodic structure.

PQC is designed around problems where this structure is absent or not known to be exploitable in the same way.

For example:

- lattice-based schemes rely on hard problems in high-dimensional lattices
- hash-based signatures rely on one-way hash functions
- code-based schemes rely on decoding problems in error-correcting codes

This does not mean PQC is magically guaranteed forever. Cryptography always depends on ongoing analysis, attacks, implementation quality, and careful standardisation. But it does mean that PQC is designed specifically to avoid the known quantum attacks that threaten RSA and ECC.

## What This Means for HPC

HPC systems are already built with strong security controls such as network separation, identity management, access control, monitoring, and encrypted communication.

The quantum security issue does not mean that supercomputers suddenly become unusable. Instead, it means that the cryptographic infrastructure around HPC will eventually need to evolve.

This could affect:

- SSH authentication
- TLS and HTTPS connections
- VPNs
- signed software packages
- research data transfer
- long-term archival data
- identity and access management

For HPC users, this is important because many workflows depend on secure remote access and trusted data movement. As PQC adoption grows, some of the tools used in research computing may gradually move toward quantum-resistant algorithms.

## Reflection

What I found interesting is how this topic connects several areas I care about: high-performance computing, mathematics, cybersecurity, and future computing technologies.

At first, I thought the issue was simply that quantum computers might be able to “break security.” But the more accurate explanation is that quantum computers threaten specific mathematical assumptions behind current public-key cryptography.

This also made me appreciate how much of computing depends on invisible foundations. When I use SSH to access an HPC system, or when data moves securely between systems, there is a whole cryptographic layer underneath. Most of the time, I do not think about it. But quantum computing shows that these foundations are not permanent; they need to evolve with technology.

Studying HPC makes this feel more concrete. Supercomputing centres are not only about raw computation. They also need secure access, reliable infrastructure, data protection, and long-term trust. Quantum-safe cryptography is likely to become part of that future.

## Summary

The issue is that sufficiently powerful quantum computers could break widely used public-key cryptography such as RSA and ECC.

Shor’s algorithm is central to this risk because it can solve the mathematical problems behind RSA and ECC much faster than classical algorithms. Post-Quantum Cryptography addresses this by using different mathematical foundations that are believed to resist known quantum attacks.

For HPC, the implication is that authentication, encrypted communication, and research data protection will eventually need to adapt to a post-quantum security model.
