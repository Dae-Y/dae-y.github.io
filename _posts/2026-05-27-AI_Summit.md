Reflection on AI for Good Innovation Factory Australia 2026


<div style="text-align: left; margin-bottom: 5px;">

A little while ago, I received a LinkedIn message from Peter about this event. He mentioned that, since I seemed interested in AI, I might want to have a look at it.

When I searched it up, I found that the AI for Good Innovation Factory Australia 2026 event was being held on Wednesday evening, 27 May, in Perth City. It looked like a good opportunity, so I decided to attend.

At first, I thought it would simply be a local AI startup event. However, once I arrived, I realised it was actually a much bigger event than I expected. It was the Australian chapter of the AI for Good Innovation Factory, a global startup pitching and acceleration platform connected to the International Telecommunication Union's AI for Good initiative. Five Australian AI startup finalists were pitching, and one winner would represent Australia at the Grand Finale in Geneva, Switzerland, during the AI for Good Global Summit.

Listening to all five finalists was genuinely impressive. Each startup had a very different focus, but they were all trying to use AI for real-world impact.

In the order I remember them, these were some of my thoughts.

Enli focused on healthcare decision support and reconstructing patient health states from complex real-world data. What stood out to me was the importance of clean, well-structured medical data. In healthcare AI, the model itself is important, but the quality and preparation of the underlying data can be just as critical. Their pitch reminded me again that AI in medicine is not just about building a powerful model. It is also about making messy clinical information usable, reliable, and safe.

Enterprise Monkey presented an idea around collaborative AI agents and an open-source AI think tank called Agents for Humanity. I found this concept interesting because it frames AI agents not just as individual assistants, but as part of a larger multi-layer system for solving complex global challenges. It made me think about how multi-agent systems might be used to structure collective intelligence at scale.

IDAi presented a deepfake detection and content verification platform. Their strength seemed to come from specialised datasets and the ability to detect synthetic media in a world where digital trust is becoming harder to maintain. Since I am currently doing Honours research related to medical image segmentation, I found myself wondering about the technical side as well. For example, I was curious whether parts of their system involve pixel-level analysis, segmentation-style approaches, or vision transformer-based architectures.

RadiXplore focused on geoscience and mining data. Their pitch was one of the most personally interesting to me because they talked about the value hidden inside underutilised historical geological data, including old reports, PDFs, and legacy documents. The idea was that a lot of valuable knowledge already exists, but it is locked away in formats that are difficult to search, connect, and use. AI and LLMs become much more useful when this legacy information can be converted into a form that machines can actually process.

Valearnis presented Project Socrates, an AI-powered educational engine that uses adaptive Socratic dialogue and multi-model AI debate to support critical thinking. I also liked this idea. Everyone knows that general-purpose AI tools can hallucinate, give incorrect answers, or become difficult to use safely in education without careful design. Using AI to help students think, question, and reason rather than simply receive answers is a much better direction.

Overall, I felt that all five finalists were strong. Each team had a clear problem space and a meaningful reason for using AI. The five-minute pitch format felt very short, and I wished there had been more time to ask questions.

For Enterprise Monkey, I wanted to ask more about the practical side of multi-agent systems: token usage, cost, latency, and how long it takes for a user to receive a final answer if multiple agents are interacting with each other in sequence.

For IDAi, I wanted to ask more about their dataset and technical approach to deepfake detection. I was especially curious about how they analyse manipulated visual content and whether their approach uses pixel-wise inspection, segmentation-like methods, vision transformers, or other computer vision techniques.

For RadiXplore, I found the connection to legacy documents especially relevant to something I have been thinking about recently. Although their scope is mining and geoscience, the broader problem appears in many domains.

These days, ordinary users use tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and NotebookLM by uploading text, PDFs, Word documents, or other readable files, then asking questions about them. But a lot of real-world information is not always stored in clean, AI-friendly formats.

For example, my father has spent years keeping old emails and documents for personal and administrative matters. When he needs to revisit those archived emails, the information is technically there, but it is not always easy to search, summarise, or ask questions about it. Many of these files are stored in formats such as `.msg` and `.eml`, which are much less convenient to use with modern AI tools compared to plain text, Markdown, or PDF.

This is one reason I have recently been developing a small side project around converting archived email files into a format that is easier to search, summarise, and use with LLMs. Once it goes beyond the MVP stage, I may write a separate blog post about it.

The broader point is this: whether or not AI ever reaches human-level intelligence, AI and LLMs are already powerful tools right now. Many people are already using them actively and practically.

Because of that, I think there will be growing value in businesses and tools that prepare information into an AI-ready form. Whether it is healthcare data, mining data, old PDFs, historical reports, or archived emails, the pattern is similar. The information often already exists, but it needs to be cleaned, structured, and transformed before AI can use it well.

That is also what I am trying to explore with `.msg` and `.eml` files: taking information that is technically available, but not very AI-friendly, and converting it into Markdown or another clean format that can be used more easily with AI tools.

So it was exciting to hear pitches that aligned with some of my own recent thoughts.

Another thing I was reminded of is how difficult it is to give a good pitch. I felt the same thing when I participated in hackathons as an undergraduate Computer Science student, and also when I presented the work from my Pawsey/CSIRO research internship.

It is very hard to compress months or years of effort, technical detail, and passion into five minutes. A good pitch is not just about having a good idea. It is also about communicating clearly, choosing what to leave out, and helping the audience understand why the work matters.

As someone who speaks English as a second language, clear technical communication is still something I am actively working to improve. I want to become better at communicating my genuine interest, effort, and ideas in a way that others can clearly understand.

There were also two student team presentations near the end of the event. I do not know their exact school year, but seeing students in school uniforms present their ideas so clearly was honestly impressive. They defined their problem statements, conducted interviews, connected their ideas to technology, and delivered their presentations with confidence. It made me wonder whether I could have done something like that at their age.

I am glad I attended this event. It was valuable to see how AI startups are applying their ideas to real-world problems across various areas. It also gave me a lot to think about in relation to my own research, and communication skills.

Thank you again to Peter for sharing the event with me. I also hope the organisers, sponsors, speakers, finalists, student teams, and everyone involved continue to do well.

Thanks for reading.
