A short reflection on my first internship.

The internship began in November 2025, shortly after I completed my Semester 2 exams. I took a brief trip to Korea and then returned to Perth to begin my summer internship.

Through CSIRO, I had the opportunity to work as a research intern at the Pawsey Supercomputing Research Centre.

This was my first professional internship related to Computer Science, and looking back, I feel very fortunate to have received the opportunity.

Throughout 2025, I spent a significant amount of time looking for internships and graduate roles. I wanted practical experience that would allow me to apply what I had learned at university and contribute to a meaningful project.

I submitted approximately 50 applications. Most of them resulted in rejection emails, but I continued applying while studying, working, attending industry events and webinars, updating my résumé, and completing additional training such as an AWS certification.

Eventually, that consistent effort led to a call from Pawsey.

The internship programme was very well organised. It began with a week of training, which allowed the interns to learn about the organisation, its research environment, and the systems we would be using before fully beginning our projects.

During the internship, I was introduced to several areas that were either new to me or that I had previously only encountered at a basic level. These included high-performance computing, computational chemistry, agentic AI, research software, and scientific communication.

Working on a chemistry-focused AI agent allowed me to experience the challenges of developing a system for a specialised domain. It was not enough to simply connect a language model to a set of tools and expect the system to work reliably.

The project required careful consideration of workflow design, software structure, testing, model behaviour, and how scientific information should be represented and validated.

This experience reinforced something I had begun to notice through university projects: building a useful AI system involves much more than generating code.

The internship also included workshops for students, including sessions on how to communicate scientific research effectively. I appreciated that the programme invested time not only in technical training, but also in helping interns explain their work clearly to people from different backgrounds.

I met researchers, software developers, other interns, and people working across several scientific disciplines. Being surrounded by people with different areas of expertise was one of the most valuable parts of the experience.

Through CSIRO, I was also able to access O’Reilly courses on topics such as agentic AI and software architecture. My degree has given me strong foundations in computer science, but I wanted to further strengthen my knowledge of areas such as software testing, system design, and architecture.

The internship gave me a practical reason to explore those topics more deeply.

It also changed how I think about AI-assisted software development.

Modern coding agents can generate code quickly, explain unfamiliar systems, and automate parts of the development process. They can make individual developers significantly more productive, particularly when completing repetitive or well-defined tasks.

However, my experience showed me that producing code is only one part of developing a reliable system.

Someone still needs to understand the requirements, design the architecture, evaluate the output, identify incorrect assumptions, test the system, manage security risks, and take responsibility for the final result.

AI may reduce the amount of routine coding required for some tasks, including work that has traditionally been given to junior developers. At the same time, this may increase the value of skills such as system design, validation, domain knowledge, communication, and accountability.

For that reason, I do not believe that software engineering is simply disappearing. I think the role is changing.

Developers may spend less time writing every line manually and more time supervising automated tools, integrating systems, evaluating results, and making higher-level technical decisions.

It is difficult to predict how far this change will go. Even if AI systems eventually become capable of completing most technical tasks independently, questions of responsibility, governance, safety, and legal accountability will remain important.

For now, the internship has encouraged me to keep developing both my AI knowledge and my broader software engineering foundations.

More importantly, it reminded me why I wanted professional experience in the first place.

During my Capstone project and this internship, I found that I felt most fulfilled when something I had learned could be used to help another person or contribute to a larger project.

After many unsuccessful applications, receiving this opportunity made the experience even more meaningful. It showed me that small and consistent efforts can eventually lead somewhere, even when progress is not immediately visible.

I am grateful to CSIRO, Pawsey, my supervisors, and everyone involved in the internship programme.

It was an experience I will not forget, and it gave me a stronger foundation as I begin my Computing Honours year in 2026.
