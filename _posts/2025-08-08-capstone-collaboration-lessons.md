It is August 2025, and I am now a few weeks into the second semester of my final year.

One of the biggest lessons I have learned this year is that building software as a team is very different from building something alone.

During Capstone Project 1, much of our work focused on planning and software construction practices. We worked on documentation, Agile processes, client meetings, requirements, and the early design of our application. It was interesting to see how the ideas discussed in university classes were applied to a real team project.

At that stage, I thought the development phase would be relatively straightforward once we began writing the code.

Capstone Project 2 showed me otherwise.

Our team consisted of six people working across the frontend, backend, database, and supporting documentation. As development progressed, different parts of the application began to overlap. Code was changed in multiple places, branches fell behind, and Git conflicts appeared more often than I expected.

I had used Git and Docker before, but using them individually was very different from coordinating them across an entire team. A command that worked perfectly on my computer did not necessarily solve the broader problem of keeping everyone’s environment and branches consistent.

At first, I saw merge conflicts mainly as a technical issue. Over time, I realised that many of them were also communication issues.

It was important to know not only how to resolve a conflict, but also:

* who was currently working on each component
* which branch contained the latest version
* whether a change affected the frontend, backend, or both
* when changes should be reviewed and merged
* how clearly tasks and progress were recorded

This helped me understand why professional teams place so much emphasis on communication, task management, code reviews, and clearly defined responsibilities.

Tools such as Git, Docker, and Jira are useful, but the tools alone do not create an effective workflow. A team still needs to communicate consistently and agree on how those tools should be used.

I also learned more about my own working style.

I tend to focus deeply on solving the problem in front of me, but a team project requires stepping back and making sure that everyone has the same understanding. Sometimes it is better to ask a question early, write a clearer explanation, or have a short conversation before beginning a large change.

As someone who is naturally quiet, this did not always come easily to me. However, I found that I genuinely enjoyed helping teammates, reviewing changes, resolving issues, and watching the different parts of the application come together.

The most rewarding moments were not necessarily when I finished my own code. They were when the team solved a shared problem and the application started working as a complete system.

There were frustrating moments, but I do not see them as failures. They were practical lessons that would have been difficult to learn from an individual assignment.

The project taught me that collaboration is not an additional skill placed on top of programming. It is a central part of software development itself.

Technical knowledge still matters, but so do the abilities to explain decisions, listen to others, manage changes, and take responsibility for the effect your work has on the rest of the system.

I still have a lot to improve, especially in communication, Git workflows, software architecture, and project coordination. However, I now have a much clearer understanding of why these skills matter.

Capstone has been challenging, but that is also what has made it valuable. It has shown me that becoming a better developer is not only about writing better code. It is also about becoming someone other people can work with and rely on.
