Attended another ACS Tech Connect event this week, titled **The Anti-AI Hour: Rethinking the AI Hype**.

Although it was organised by ACS Victoria, I was glad that virtual attendance was available.

The event was called the Anti-AI Hour, but it was not really about opposing AI. It was more about stepping back from the constant hype and having a realistic discussion about what AI can currently do, where it still struggles, and how it may affect jobs and businesses.

I liked the use of a QR code that allowed attendees to submit comments and controversial AI opinions. The panel could then respond to ideas from both the in-person and online audience.

It made the event feel more interactive than a standard online presentation. Some of the audience topics included whether SaaS is dying, whether AI is making people lazy, whether it will remove jobs, and whether people are becoming too dependent on AI-generated answers.

The discussion about AI making people lazy was especially relatable.

AI can reduce the mental effort needed to begin a task. Starting from a blank page is often difficult, but once AI produces a draft, it becomes much easier to identify what is incorrect, reorganise the content, and add your own ideas.

At the same time, there is a risk of accepting an answer simply because it sounds confident. Using multiple AI models and receiving similar answers does not necessarily mean that the answer is correct.

For me, the useful middle ground is to use AI to reduce repetitive work while still applying my own judgement. The panel made a similar point: the value of using AI depends on the task and on which parts of the work genuinely require human judgement, nuance, or domain knowledge.

Another part that caught my attention was the discussion about **harness engineering**.

Interestingly, this was also a topic at the KDA meetup I attended on 25 June at the Microsoft building in Southbank. Hearing it mentioned again at another event made me feel that it is becoming an increasingly important direction in practical AI development.

A harness helps an AI system complete a long or complex task by breaking it into clearer stages, maintaining context, recording progress, and allowing another agent or context window to continue from where the previous one stopped.

Without this structure, an agent may complete only a small part of the task and incorrectly claim that everything is finished. It may also lose track of the original goal as the conversation becomes longer.

The idea itself is not completely new. In software engineering, we already divide large systems into smaller components, define workflows, record state, test outputs, and handle failures. Harness engineering applies similar thinking to AI agents.

What makes it valuable is that a good harness also requires domain knowledge. Someone needs to understand the actual process well enough to divide it into meaningful stages and define what a successful output looks like.

This connected with another useful point from the panel: businesses cannot automate processes that they are unable to clearly describe.

Before adding AI, an organisation still needs to understand its data, workflows, responsibilities, and risks. Simply purchasing an “AI for everything” platform does not automatically solve those problems.

The panel also discussed accountability. Even when AI can technically perform a task, businesses must decide whether they are willing to accept responsibility when the system makes a mistake.

This is one reason I do not think human roles will disappear as quickly or as completely as some predictions suggest. Organisations may need fewer people for certain repetitive tasks, but judgement, validation, communication, security, and accountability will remain important.

My favourite takeaway from the event was the importance of having your own opinion.

As AI becomes better at summarising information and producing reasonable-sounding answers, it becomes more important to think independently and decide whether an output is actually useful or correct.

For students and emerging professionals, simply knowing how to use an AI tool may not be enough. We also need technical foundations, domain knowledge, and the confidence to question the output.

Overall, this was another valuable event. I liked that the discussion did not treat AI as either a magical solution or something to fear. It focused instead on where the technology is useful, where the hype goes too far, and what people can do to stay adaptable.

It was also interesting to hear harness engineering discussed again so soon after the KDA meetup.

AI models will continue to improve, but practical AI systems still depend heavily on how humans structure the task, manage the context, evaluate the results, and take responsibility for the final outcome.
